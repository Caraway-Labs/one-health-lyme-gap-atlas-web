Epidemiologists use this page to move from released One Health evidence to a bounded investigation question.

Atlas follows this path:

`DATA → SIGNALS → CONTEXT → INTERPRETATION → INVESTIGATION → PUBLIC-HEALTH ACTION`

Each step answers a different question. A visually prominent value is not a complete conclusion.

<Callout title="Interpret with care" type="warn">
  Data availability is not disease absence. A missing, suppressed, stale, or
  incomparable value stays different from a measured zero. A signal focuses
  attention. It is not a final epidemiological conclusion.
</Callout>

## 1. Data — what is available?

Check the source, geography, time period, update context, and limitations in the current release. A later governed source is intended to enter through a source-specific adapter and still be read as canonical Atlas evidence, with that source's provenance attached. See [sources and canonical evidence](/docs/sources-and-canonical-evidence).

## 2. Signals — what stands out?

Patterns, ranked counties, and review-priority scores focus attention. Review the inputs and the methodology version. Do not infer that a higher score is a probability, incidence rate, individual risk, or disease-burden estimate unless the evidence and method explicitly say so.

## 3. Context — what else should be considered?

Read vector, pathogen, environmental, population, and scientific context with the signal. Association or co-occurrence alone does not establish causation.

## 4. Interpretation — what does the evidence support?

Check geography, time, release currency, definition, denominator, and method. Note missing, suppressed, sparse, stale, or unavailable values. A safe line is conditional: “This area is worth reviewing because these available signals align,” plus the limitations to verify.

## 5. Investigation — what should happen next?

Ask one bounded question, such as which source or time period would reduce the most uncertainty. Record geography, release or vintage, methodology, evidence, and unresolved limitations.

## 6. Public-health action — what is appropriate?

Atlas can help a team decide what deserves investigation. It does not authorize an intervention or make a public-health decision automatically. Action belongs to the responsible epidemiologists, agencies, and partners.

## A repeatable review pattern

1. **Select** a supported geography in [Atlas Explorer](/explore).
2. **Scan** the signal and the equivalent table or text.
3. **Inspect** evidence, provenance, methodology, and limitations.
4. **Compare** only aligned measures.
5. **State uncertainty.** Keep missing evidence distinct from a measured zero.
6. **Write** the question and the next evidence needed.
7. **Share** the URL or export with that context.

Boundaries are in [What is One Health Atlas?](/docs/what-is-atlas) and [Who is One Health Atlas for?](/docs/who-is-atlas-for).

## Where the signed-in workspace places each step

In the signed-in workspace, that path uses the six destinations:

1. **Atlas Explorer** holds the operating picture for the United States or one state, plus county evidence and suggested follow-up on the same address.
2. **Compare** holds two counties. It does not rank them.
3. **Ask Atlas** is the dedicated page for reviewed-literature questions when that service is enabled. It does not open a side panel.
4. **Feed**, **Settings**, and **Docs** stay their own destinations. Feed lists recent items from approved trusted sources when the service returns them. Settings stores a default jurisdiction. Docs opens in a new tab and leaves the workspace URL on the original tab.

Share the workspace URL for the page you mean to hand off. Read [the professional workspace](/docs/professional-workspace) for addresses, evidence states, and older bookmarks.
